Stop If Unclear

The safety net most teams reach for is a confidence check. Models are often confidently wrong, so the check stops on the harmless cases and waves the expensive ones straight through.

Part 1 ended with a line. Below it, the agent computes: it moves data across messy systems and applies the rule you already wrote. Above it, a human decides. Part 1 also ended with the question the whole design depends on. Something has to put each case on the right side of that line, one case at a time, at the speed of the queue. What does that?

Ask a team, and the answer usually comes fast.

"The agent will escalate when it's not sure."

It sounds like engineering. There is a score, a threshold, and someone to tune it. It is also the intro's sentence again: common sense will be applied, now by the model, on request. It fails in one direction, and that direction is the expensive one.

Confidence tracks the text

A model's confidence mostly measures how familiar the input looks. It is high when the message resembles millions of messages the model has seen and the answer follows a well-worn path. It drops when the phrasing is odd, the formatting is broken, the language is rare, or the customer takes nine paragraphs to reach the point.

None of those things is about money. None is about trust, precedent, or whether the action can be undone tomorrow. Confidence carries some signal about whether the answer is right. It carries almost none about what a wrong answer costs.

So the gate misfires in both directions. A furious customer writes in fragments about a €9 shipping fee. The message reads as unclear, the agent stops, and a person spends real time on nine euros. A polite customer sends a tidy refund request, and it is their sixth fraudulent one this year. That message reads as clear, because it is clear. It is fluent, ordinary, on-policy, and wrong. The model has no doubt, so it reports none.

The agent stops on the cases that are hard to read. You need it to stop on the cases that are expensive to get wrong. Those two sets barely overlap.

Both misfires cost you, and they feed each other. The escalation queue fills with the bottom-right box: messy, cheap, done in seconds. The person working that queue learns, correctly, that a flagged case is usually nothing. The top-left box never reaches her at all. The mechanism that hides the dangerous cases is the same one teaching her to skim the cases she does see.

Go back to the two cases from the intro, the ones that arrive looking identical. Case A is a first-time customer who forgot to cancel. Case B is the sixth forgotten trial this year across three accounts, with the same wording each time. A confidence gate treats them the same way, and on the text alone that is correct: both messages are equally clear. What separates them is the history. The model never saw the history, so it never had a reason to doubt.

The supervisor trap

The usual next move is a second model that checks the first. The appeal is real. It is cheap, it adds milliseconds, it needs no headcount, and it produces a label for the dashboard: verified.

Look at what the second model receives. It gets the same message, the same policy text and, in most builds, the same missing history. It was trained on the same kind of data and shares the same priors. When two checkers read the same input and lack the same facts, their mistakes line up. The supervisor objects where the first model was already hesitant, which is the noise. It agrees where the first model was confidently wrong, which is the damage. The second vote comes from a voter with the same information and the same blind spot.

The arithmetic behind the pitch
The case for a supervisor is multiplication. If the agent is wrong on 1% of cases and the supervisor misses 5% of those errors, 1 case in 2,000 slips through. That figure holds only if the two models fail independently. Two models that read the same input and lack the same facts do not. The real figure depends on how often they fail on the same cases, and you can measure that on your own traffic. Until you measure it, 1 in 2,000 is an assumption with a decimal point.

The second-order effect arrives later. People downstream read verified as checked by someone. The label lowers the attention of the one reviewer in the system who really is independent: the human. Suppose the supervisor catches a third of the errors, and the label halves how closely people look. You can end up with more errors reaching customers and a dashboard that shows fewer. That trade is measurable. Measure it before the label ships.

A model that watches model output still has a job. I have written about that job before: catching drift, broken tone, and policy wording that wanders over weeks. The evidence for those problems is in the text, so a model finds it well. Judgment about this particular customer needs facts that the text does not contain. A second reader of the same text cannot supply them.

Ask the case, not the model

The fix is to stop asking the agent how sure it feels and start asking what the case is. A good escalation trigger has three properties. It is computed from data the agent did not generate. It gives the same answer every time for the same case. And a person can read it in one line and agree or disagree. Five questions cover most of the ground.

Reversibility
Can a person undo this tomorrow with one click? Or does it move money, close an account, or send a message that cannot be recalled?
Magnitude
How does the amount compare with the usual amount for this product, this segment, this week?
Pattern
Has this customer, device, address, or payment method done this before?
Novelty
Does this case resemble one already decided, or is it the first of its kind?
Value at risk
What is this relationship worth, and what does a wrong answer cost beyond this one transaction?

None of these questions needs a model to answer it. Every answer leaves an audit trail. Each one has a threshold, and a named person sets it.

Here is Case B again, run through both gates.

The case
A polite, well-written request to refund a €180 annual charge after an auto-converted trial. The wording follows the policy. The text contains no warning signs.
Confidence gate
Confidence 0.97. The gate passes it, and the agent acts in under a second.
Stakes gate
Before the agent acts, a query runs: three accounts, one device, the sixth trial refund in eleven months. The pattern trigger fires. The case goes to the exception owner with the history attached.
The difference
The warning sign was never in the message. It was in a table the model could not see, and the stakes gate read that table.
The rule that replaces "stop if unclear"
Escalation is a property of the case. If your trigger cannot run while the model is switched off, it is not a trigger. It is a hope.

Notice who owns the thresholds. They are not a technical setting, and the engineer tuning the pipeline should not choose them alone. Reversibility, magnitude, and value at risk are the parameters from Part 1, and they belong to the parameter owner. This is the line from Part 1's anatomy, written down in numbers.

The switch also changes what escalation volume is. Under a confidence gate, the number of cases that reach people is an output. It is whatever the model found strange this week, and it moves every time the vendor updates the model. Under stakes triggers, it is an input, and you set it. Tighten the thresholds and more cases reach people: cost goes up and risk goes down. Loosen them and the reverse happens. That makes escalation volume a budget. A budget has a price per unit, and that price is the number this series ends on.

Keep the confidence score, and give it a smaller job. Low-confidence cases cluster around inputs your process handles badly, so they make a useful pile for review and for fixing the process. They make a poor gate between an agent and a customer's money.

What this costs you

Two things get harder before they get easier.

First, someone has to write the triggers down. "The agent will escalate when it's not sure" requires no one to define anything, and that is a large part of why it survives design reviews. Stakes triggers make the parameter owner state what counts as consequential, in numbers, on paper, under their own name. That is the discomfort this series ends on, arriving early and in a smaller dose.

Second, a trigger list catches only the kinds of trouble you already know. A new fraud pattern passes every rule you wrote, and it passes every confidence check too. No gate fixes this. What finds it is the audit wire from Part 1: a random sample of resolved cases, read by a person, picked without looking at any score. That sample shows you what your rules do not describe yet. On a dashboard it looks like waste. In practice it is where next quarter's triggers come from.

What comes next

Now the routing is deliberate. Cases reach people because of what they are, and the people above the line get the dozen cases that carry the weight.

That creates the next problem, and it is a human one. Give a person a queue of flagged cases and an approve button. Sooner or later, clicking approve becomes faster than reading. The human in the loop turns into a signature on the loop. The org chart shows accountability, and the practice is a rubber stamp. That is more dangerous than an empty chair, because everyone downstream believes the case was read.

The fix runs against intuition: send people fewer cases, not more.

That is Part 3.
